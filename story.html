The Bulletin, 1892

The Story

One night, one snake, and a lifetime of hardship remembered in flashes.

“The Drover’s Wife” opens not with its human subject but with her surroundings, and Lawson makes sure we feel how little there is to hold onto:

“The two-roomed house is built of round timber, slabs, and stringy-bark, and floored with split slabs. A big bark kitchen standing at one end is larger than the house itself… Bush all round — bush with no horizon.”

The setup

The drover has been away for months, working the droving routes with the sheep, as he does for most of the year. His wife is left to run the hut alone with their four children and a half-wild dog named Alligator. Money is scarce, help is nonexistent, and the nearest town is nineteen miles off. It is, in every practical sense, just her.

That evening, one of the children spots a black snake slide in under the two-roomed house through a crack in the slab floor. There is no husband to send for, no neighbour close enough to call. She sends the children up onto the table, blocks the crack as best she can, arms herself, and settles in to wait the snake out — all night if it takes that long.

A life measured in emergencies

As the night stretches on, the story drifts, in short remembered vignettes, through the disasters she has already survived alone: a bushfire that nearly took the house, fought back with wet bags; a flood that forced her to carry the children to high ground; a mad bullock that charged the hut for a day and a night before wandering off; a plague of crows tormenting a dying sheep; and a swagman who came to the door with a manner she didn’t trust, and whom she saw off without ever quite letting him know how frightened she was.

None of these are told as heroics. Lawson’s narration is flat, understated, almost matter-of-fact — which is exactly what makes the accumulated weight of them land so hard. This isn’t a woman who rises to one dramatic occasion. It is a woman for whom crisis is simply Tuesday.

The watch

Through the night she nods off despite herself and startles awake; she tells the children stories to keep them calm; she keeps the fire built up and the gun within reach. Alligator, restless, prowls the edges of the room, certain something is wrong before anyone else is. It is the dog, in the end, who corners and kills the snake as dawn is breaking, dragging it out where the family can finally see the thing that kept them awake all night.

The ending

With the danger over, the exhausted woman pulls her youngest son Tommy close. He has stayed awake with her through the worst of it, gun-shy and clumsy but trying hard to be the man of the house in his father’s absence. He looks at the dead snake, then at his mother, and makes her a promise:

“Mother, I won’t never go drovin’; blast me if I do!”

She hugs him and says nothing to correct him. It is a small, ambiguous grace note: on one hand, hope that the cycle of absence and isolation might end with this generation; on the other, the reader already half-knows how bush life tends to go. Lawson doesn’t resolve that tension — he just lets the sun come up.

At a glance

Fast facts

  • First published in The Bulletin, 1892
  • Collected in While the Billy Boils, 1896
  • Setting: an unnamed, isolated station in the NSW bush
  • Cast: the wife, her four children (Tommy is named), the dog Alligator, and a black snake
  • The husband never appears — only his absence does
  • Roughly 3,500 words; one continuous night
