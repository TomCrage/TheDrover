1892 — today

Legacy & Adaptations

Few Australian short stories have been painted, rewritten, and reclaimed as often as this one.

“The Drover’s Wife” didn’t stay still. Across the twentieth century and into the twenty-first, painters, novelists, playwrights, and filmmakers kept returning to the woman in the hut — each finding something Lawson left unsaid.

1892

Henry Lawson, “The Drover’s Wife”

First published in The Bulletin, then collected in While the Billy Boils (1896). The original text: spare, understated, and quietly radical for centring a nameless bushwoman rather than the drover himself.

1945

Russell Drysdale, The Drover’s Wife (painting)

Drysdale’s modernist painting, now held by the National Gallery of Australia, reimagines Lawson’s heroine as a stark, monumental figure standing in a bare ochre landscape. It became one of the defining images of twentieth-century Australian art, and cemented the drover’s wife as a national symbol in her own right — visual shorthand, almost, for the whole idea of the bush.

1970s

Murray Bail, “The Drover’s Wife” (short story)

Bail’s metafictional response takes Drysdale’s painting, not Lawson’s text, as its starting point. A husband insists the woman in the painting is his own runaway wife, staring down at us across the decades — a playful, unsettling meditation on who gets to claim a story, and what a picture can and can’t prove. Other writers, including Frank Moorhouse and Barbara Jefferis, wrote their own rival versions around the same period, turning “the drover’s wife” into a kind of shared literary character that Australian authors kept borrowing and answering back to.

2016

Leah Purcell, The Drover’s Wife (stage play)

Purcell — a Goa-Gunggari-Wakka Wakka Murri writer, actor, and director — premiered her play at Belvoir St Theatre in Sydney, writing the lead role for herself. Her version keeps Lawson’s bare bush setting but pulls colonial violence and First Nations history into the foreground, reframing the isolated hut as a site of frontier conflict as much as domestic endurance.

2019

Leah Purcell, The Drover’s Wife: The Legend of Molly Johnson (novel)

Purcell expanded the play into a novel, naming her protagonist Molly Johnson and deepening the frontier-violence narrative introduced on stage — a heroine forged from Lawson’s original but no longer bound by it.

2021

Leah Purcell, The Drover’s Wife: The Legend of Molly Johnson (film)

Purcell wrote, directed, and starred in the film adaptation, completing the journey from Lawson’s few thousand words to a full-scale reclamation of the story — a century and change after “the drover’s wife” first went unnamed on the page.

Why she keeps getting rewritten

An empty space every generation fills in

Lawson left the drover’s wife without a name, a face, or a clear future. That absence turned out to be an invitation. Drysdale gave her a monumental form; Bail gave her a disputed identity; Purcell gave her a name, a history, and a reckoning with the violence of colonial Australia that Lawson’s original barely gestures toward. Each retelling says less about what Lawson wrote than about what each new era needed the story to mean.
