1867 – 1922

Henry Lawson

Bush balladeer, Bulletin writer, and one of Australia’s most celebrated literary figures.

A childhood in the goldfields

Henry Lawson was born on 17 June 1867 at Grenfell, on the New South Wales goldfields, the son of a Norwegian-born miner, Niels Hertzberg Larsen (whose surname was anglicised to Lawson), and Louisa Lawson, who would go on to become a writer, publisher, and feminist campaigner in her own right — founding and editing The Dawn, one of Australia’s earliest women’s newspapers.

Around the age of nine, an ear infection left Henry Lawson increasingly, and eventually almost totally, deaf. It cut him off from easy conversation for the rest of his life and is often pointed to as one source of the acute, watchful isolation that runs through so much of his writing — including “The Drover’s Wife.”

The Bulletin years

In the 1890s Lawson became one of the leading voices of the nationalist bush-realist writing associated with The Bulletin magazine in Sydney, alongside contemporaries such as Banjo Paterson. Where Paterson’s bush was often adventurous and romantic, Lawson pushed back with a harder, plainer picture of rural hardship — the two even traded verses in print debating exactly this difference. “The Drover’s Wife,” published in The Bulletin in 1892 and later collected in his landmark volume While the Billy Boils (1896), is Lawson’s case for his side of that argument.

Hardship and recognition

Lawson’s own life was marked by poverty, alcoholism, and periods of institutionalisation; his marriage to Bertha Bredt ended in separation, and he spent time in Darlinghurst Gaol over debt-related matters. Despite this, by the time of his death he was already regarded as a defining Australian literary voice, celebrated for capturing ordinary bush life in a plain, unsentimental style that felt genuinely new.

Henry Lawson died on 2 September 1922 in Sydney. He was given a state funeral — the first Australian writer to be so honoured — and his image later appeared on the Australian ten-dollar note, a measure of how central he remains to the country’s sense of its own literary tradition.
