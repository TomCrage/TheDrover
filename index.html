The Drover’s Wife

Alone in a two-roomed bush hut with four children, a loaded gun, and a dog called Alligator, she keeps watch through the night — one hardship in a long, unflinching life of them. A story of isolation and quiet endurance that has haunted Australian storytelling for more than a century.

Where it begins

A hut, a snake, and a night that won’t end

Henry Lawson published “The Drover’s Wife” in The Bulletin in 1892, and later collected it in While the Billy Boils (1896). It is barely a few thousand words long, and it is still one of the most anthologised, most taught, and most argued-over short stories in Australian literature.

On the surface, almost nothing happens: a woman spends one long night guarding her sleeping children from a snake that has slipped under the floorboards of an isolated bush hut. But Lawson uses that single, tense vigil as a frame for something much larger — a whole unspoken life of bushfires, floods, loneliness, and quiet, unglamorous courage, lived out while her husband is away droving sheep for months at a time.

Why it still matters

A story with no name for its hero

Lawson never gives the drover’s wife a name. She is defined entirely by her relationship to an absent man and by the work of survival itself — which is precisely the point. Where earlier Australian bush writing tended to romanticise the pioneer or the drover as a heroic wanderer, Lawson turns the camera around: the real endurance test isn’t out on the droving track, it’s the one happening at home, alone, night after night.

That quiet reversal is why the story has outlived its century. It has been read as an early, unglamorous portrait of bush womanhood; painted as a monument by Russell Drysdale; rewritten as metafiction by Murray Bail; and, most dramatically, reclaimed by Leah Purcell, who folded questions of colonial violence and First Nations history back into the landscape Lawson described but never fully saw.
